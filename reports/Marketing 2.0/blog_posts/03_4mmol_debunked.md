## Introduction: The Line That Won't Die

Open almost any lactate testing software on the market. Upload a step test. Watch the graph render. And there it is --- a horizontal line drawn at exactly 4.0 mmol/L of blood lactate, slicing across the chart like a verdict.

That line is supposed to mark your lactate threshold. The intensity above which lactate accumulates faster than your body can clear it. The boundary between sustainable and unsustainable effort. The single most important number in endurance training prescription.

There is one problem: it is wrong for most athletes.

Not wrong by a little. Wrong by an amount that changes training zones, distorts periodization, and --- in trained athletes --- systematically pushes prescribed intensities into a range that produces fatigue without adaptation.

The 4mmol fixed threshold (sometimes called OBLA, onset of blood lactate accumulation) is still the default in most commercial lactate analysis software, most coaching certification curricula, and most performance testing labs worldwide. It appears in textbooks published this decade. Coaches with decades of experience defend it. Athletes pay hundreds of dollars for tests that use it.

And it was debunked forty-five years ago.

---

## Where 4mmol Came From

To understand why 4mmol persists, you have to understand why it was invented --- and what it was invented *for*.

In 1976, a German exercise physiologist named Alois Mader and his colleagues published a paper in *Sportarzt und Sportmedizin* titled "Zur Beurteilung der sportartspezifischen Ausdauerleistungsfaehigkeit im Labor" --- roughly, "On the assessment of sport-specific endurance capacity in the laboratory" (Mader et al., 1976).

Mader's problem was practical: different labs across Germany were testing athletes with different protocols, different blood sampling methods, and different definitions of "threshold." Results were incomparable. A swimmer tested in Cologne could not be meaningfully compared to a swimmer tested in Munich.

Mader proposed a standardization: use a fixed blood lactate concentration of 4 mmol/L as a reference point. Not because 4mmol had special physiological meaning. Not because every athlete's threshold occurred at 4mmol. But because it provided a *common reference value* that any lab could identify, making cross-lab and cross-study comparisons possible.

It was a methodological convenience, not a physiological discovery.

Nine years later, Heck et al. (1985) published "Justification of the 4-mmol/l lactate threshold" in the *International Journal of Sports Medicine*. This paper is frequently cited as "proof" that 4mmol works. What it actually showed was that 4mmol correlated reasonably well with maximal lactate steady state (MLSS) in *some* populations --- specifically, a group of male athletes of mixed training status.

The key word in that finding is **some**. Heck's own data showed substantial individual variation. The correlation was a group-level statistical observation, not an individual-level diagnostic tool. Applying a group mean to an individual is a basic statistical error --- one that exercise science has been committing at scale for four decades.

## What Stegmann Showed in 1981

Just five years after Mader's original proposal, the conceptual foundation of the fixed threshold was already crumbling.

In 1981, Stegmann, Kindermann, and Schnabel published "Lactate kinetics and individual anaerobic threshold" in the *International Journal of Sports Medicine*. Their paper introduced the concept of the **individual anaerobic threshold (IAT)** --- a method that determined each athlete's threshold based on their personal lactate kinetics during and after exercise, rather than referencing a fixed concentration.

Their central finding was devastating for the 4mmol model: **the actual anaerobic threshold varied between 1.5 and 7.0 mmol/L** across their subject pool.

Read that range again. 1.5 to 7.0 mmol/L. A nearly five-fold difference.

An elite endurance athlete with years of aerobic development might reach their true threshold at 2.5 mmol/L. A powerful but aerobically underdeveloped sprinter might not hit theirs until 6.0 mmol/L. Both of these athletes, tested in a lab that uses 4mmol, would receive incorrect training zones.

Stegmann's work was not a fringe finding. It was published in one of the field's top journals. It was cited extensively. It spawned an entire lineage of individualized threshold methods. And yet, forty-five years later, the method it refuted remains the industry default.

## The Evidence Against 4mmol: Four Decades of Confirmation

Stegmann's 1981 paper was not the end of the story. It was the beginning of a long and consistent body of research showing that fixed lactate thresholds fail individual athletes.

### Faude, Kindermann & Meyer (2009) --- The Definitive Review

The most comprehensive assessment of lactate threshold concepts was published by Faude, Kindermann, and Meyer in *Sports Medicine* in 2009. This review examined over 25 studies comparing different threshold methods against the gold standard of maximal lactate steady state (MLSS).

Their conclusion was unambiguous: **"Fixed blood lactate concentrations... do not consider the individual lactate kinetics and are therefore not recommended."**

This was not a speculative opinion. It was the summary finding of a systematic review covering decades of controlled research, published in one of the highest-impact sports science journals. The paper specifically addressed 4mmol OBLA and found it inferior to individualized methods in predicting MLSS across diverse athlete populations.

If lactate threshold science were a courtroom, Faude et al. 2009 would be the verdict. The evidence was weighed, the methods were compared, and fixed thresholds were found wanting.

### Beneke et al. (2011) --- Trained Athletes Are Failed the Most

Beneke and colleagues published a detailed methodological analysis of MLSS testing in the *European Journal of Applied Physiology* in 2011. Among their findings: well-trained endurance athletes frequently demonstrate MLSS at blood lactate concentrations of **2.5 to 3.5 mmol/L** --- substantially below 4mmol.

This finding exposed the cruelest irony of the 4mmol rule. The athletes with the best aerobic development --- the ones who have invested the most time and effort into endurance training --- are the ones for whom 4mmol overestimates the threshold the most. A fixed threshold at 4mmol tells these athletes that they can sustain intensities they actually cannot. It sets their "threshold" training zone above their real threshold, converting what should be sustainable tempo work into repeated bouts of excessive lactate accumulation.

The result is predictable: chronic sympathetic overload, impaired recovery, accumulated fatigue, and performance plateaus that coaches attribute to "not training hard enough" when the real problem is training too hard at the wrong intensity.

### Coyle et al. (1988) --- Elite Cyclists Break the Model

Coyle and colleagues, in a 1988 study published in the *Journal of Applied Physiology*, examined blood lactate responses in well-trained subjects. Their data showed lactate turnpoints --- the intensity at which blood lactate begins its exponential rise --- occurring well below 4 mmol/L in highly trained individuals.

This was not surprising to physiologists familiar with training adaptations. Endurance training increases monocarboxylate transporter density, enhances mitochondrial lactate oxidation capacity, and shifts the lactate curve to the right. In a well-trained athlete, the exponential inflection point of the lactate curve might occur at 2.8 mmol/L. At 4mmol, that athlete is already deep into unsustainable territory.

### Billat et al. (2003) --- The Variation Is Too Large

Billat and colleagues published a comprehensive review of the maximal lactate steady state concept in *Sports Medicine* in 2003. Their analysis confirmed that individual variation in the blood lactate concentration at the second lactate threshold (LT2) is **too large for any single fixed value to be diagnostically useful**.

The variation is not noise. It is signal. It reflects real physiological differences in muscle fiber composition, capillary density, mitochondrial enzyme activity, monocarboxylate transporter expression, liver gluconeogenic capacity, and training history. Ignoring this variation by applying a fixed value is not simplification --- it is systematic error.

## Why 4mmol Persists

If the evidence has been clear for over four decades, why does 4mmol remain the default?

The answer is uncomfortable but simple: **it is easy**.

Drawing a horizontal line at 4mmol requires no mathematics. No curve fitting. No polynomial regression. No understanding of lactate kinetics. Any spreadsheet can do it. Any coaching certification can teach it in fifteen minutes. Any software developer can implement it in one line of code.

Individualized methods --- ModDmax, Dmax, baseline rise detection, sustained increase analysis --- require actual algorithms. They require understanding curve shape, inflection points, regression models, and confidence assessment. They require software that does real computation, not just data display.

Certification programs perpetuate 4mmol because their curricula were written decades ago and have not been updated. Software companies perpetuate it because implementing a horizontal line is cheaper than implementing curve analysis. Coaches perpetuate it because it is what they were taught, and changing practice requires admitting that years of threshold prescriptions may have been wrong.

There is also a psychological factor. A fixed value feels *certain*. "Your threshold is at 4mmol" sounds authoritative and definitive. "Your threshold is at approximately 3.1 mmol/L based on the cross-validated agreement of three independent curve-analysis methods with a confidence score of 0.82" sounds uncertain --- even though it is vastly more accurate.

The fitness industry rewards confidence over accuracy. And 4mmol is nothing if not confident.

## The Real-World Consequences

The 4mmol error is not academic. It produces measurable, harmful effects on real athletes.

### For Trained Athletes (True LT2 Below 4mmol)

This is the most common and most damaging scenario. A well-trained runner, cyclist, or triathlete walks into a lab. Their true LT2 --- the intensity above which lactate accumulation becomes unsustainable --- occurs at 3.0 mmol/L. The lab draws the 4mmol line and prescribes training zones based on that reference.

The consequences cascade:

- **Zone 2 is set too high.** What should be easy, aerobic, fat-oxidizing work becomes moderately hard glycolytic work. Recovery rides are not recovering. Easy runs are not easy.
- **Threshold work is above threshold.** Sessions prescribed at "threshold" are actually VO2max intervals. The intended metabolic stimulus is replaced by excessive stress.
- **Chronic overreaching accumulates.** The athlete trains too hard on easy days and too hard on hard days. The polarization that drives adaptation collapses into a monotonous moderate-high intensity that fatigues without developing.
- **Performance plateaus.** The coach sees stagnation and prescribes more intensity. The problem worsens.

This pattern --- overestimated threshold leading to chronic overtraining in trained athletes --- is one of the most common failure modes in endurance coaching. And the 4mmol threshold is a direct contributor.

### For Untrained or Less Aerobically Developed Athletes (True LT2 Above 4mmol)

The opposite error is less discussed but equally real. An athlete with limited aerobic training history and high glycolytic capacity might have a true LT2 at 5.5 mmol/L. Their lactate curve rises later and more steeply.

For this athlete, 4mmol falls *below* their actual threshold. Their prescribed "threshold" work is actually sub-threshold --- not hard enough to drive the adaptations they need. They spend weeks doing sessions that feel challenging but are metabolically insufficient, wondering why their race performance does not improve.

### The Irony

The athletes who need threshold precision the most --- highly trained endurance athletes competing at the margins --- are the ones 4mmol fails the most. Their years of aerobic development have shifted their lactate curves leftward, moving their real thresholds further from 4mmol. The more trained you are, the more wrong the fixed threshold becomes.

## What to Use Instead

If not 4mmol, then what? Exercise science has developed several individualized methods, each with strengths and limitations.

### ModDmax (Bishop et al., 1998)

The Modified Dmax method, published by Bishop and colleagues in *Medicine & Science in Sports & Exercise*, identifies the threshold as the point of maximum perpendicular distance between the lactate curve and a line drawn from the first significant rise in lactate to the final data point. By anchoring the start point to the first meaningful lactate increase (rather than the minimum value), ModDmax avoids the instability of the original Dmax method and produces more consistent results in trained athletes.

### Dmax (Cheng et al., 1992)

The original Dmax method, published by Cheng and colleagues in the *International Journal of Sports Medicine*, uses the maximum distance between a third-order polynomial fit of the lactate curve and a straight line connecting the first and last data points. It captures the point of greatest curvature --- the intensity at which the lactate response transitions from linear to exponential.

### Baseline Rise

This method identifies the first intensity at which blood lactate rises by a meaningful amount above baseline (resting) values. It is most useful for detecting the first lactate threshold (LT1) --- the aerobic threshold where lactate first begins to accumulate above resting concentrations.

### Sustained Increase

Rather than looking for a single jump, this method identifies the intensity at which lactate shows consecutive step-over-step increases --- a sustained upward trend rather than a transient spike. It is more robust to noise and single-point anomalies than threshold methods that rely on a single step.

### The Best Approach: Cross-Validation

No single method is perfect. Each has edge cases where it fails. The most robust approach is to **apply multiple independent methods and measure their agreement**.

When three different methods --- each using different mathematical logic, each anchored to different features of the curve --- converge on the same intensity, the confidence in that result is high. When they disagree, that disagreement is itself information: it signals an ambiguous curve, noisy data, or an athlete whose physiology sits on a boundary.

A confidence score derived from cross-method agreement is more honest and more useful than any single method's point estimate. It tells the coach not just "where is the threshold" but "how certain are we."

## The MLSS Gold Standard

Any discussion of threshold methods must reference the gold standard: **Maximal Lactate Steady State (MLSS)**.

MLSS is determined by performing multiple constant-load exercise bouts on separate days, each at a slightly different intensity, and identifying the highest intensity at which blood lactate stabilizes (does not increase by more than 1 mmol/L after the initial rise during 30 minutes of constant work). It is the true physiological definition of the threshold between sustainable and unsustainable exercise.

MLSS is impractical for routine testing. It requires 4-6 separate lab visits on different days, each involving 30+ minutes of exhausting constant-load exercise. No athlete or coach has the time or tolerance for this outside of a research setting.

But MLSS serves a critical validation role. When researchers compare threshold methods against MLSS, individualized methods (Dmax, ModDmax, IAT) consistently outperform fixed concentrations like 4mmol (Faude et al., 2009). The methods that look at curve shape match the physiological reality more closely than methods that draw horizontal lines.

## FAQ

### Is 4mmol completely useless?

Not completely. As Mader originally intended, 4mmol remains a useful **reference value for comparing athletes or populations** --- for example, tracking the pace at 4mmol across a season to monitor training response. The problem is not 4mmol as a reference; it is 4mmol as a *threshold*. Using it to define training zones for individual athletes is where the evidence clearly shows it fails.

### What should my LT2 lactate level be?

There is no universal "should." Your LT2 depends on your training history, genetics, muscle fiber composition, and sport. Highly trained endurance athletes often show LT2 between 2.5 and 3.5 mmol/L. Less aerobically developed athletes may show LT2 at 4.5-6.0 mmol/L. The only way to know yours is to test with a method that respects individual variation.

### Why does my coach still use 4mmol?

Most likely because their certification program taught it and they have not encountered the research showing its limitations. Many excellent coaches use 4mmol not out of ignorance of physiology but out of trust in their training --- which, unfortunately, has not been updated on this specific topic. Share the Faude et al. 2009 review with them. It is the most accessible summary of the evidence.

### Is OBLA the same as 4mmol?

Yes. OBLA stands for Onset of Blood Lactate Accumulation and is defined as the intensity corresponding to a blood lactate concentration of 4 mmol/L. It is the same concept under a different name. The same criticisms apply.

### How do I know my real lactate threshold?

Get a graded exercise test (step test) with blood lactate sampling at each stage. Ensure the analysis uses an individualized method --- Dmax, ModDmax, or multi-method cross-validation --- rather than a fixed 4mmol reference. If the lab report shows a horizontal line at 4mmol, you are not getting an individualized analysis.

### Can I have a threshold above 4mmol?

Absolutely. Athletes with high glycolytic capacity and limited aerobic development can have true LT2 values at 5, 6, or even 7 mmol/L. This is not abnormal; it reflects a specific physiological profile. For these athletes, 4mmol would underestimate their threshold and set training zones too low.
