## What It Does

Lactate Lab's lactate curve analysis tool takes your raw step test data — pace or power, heart rate, and blood lactate — and runs it through three independent threshold detection algorithms simultaneously. Instead of relying on a single method (which can fail depending on your fitness profile), the analyzer cross-validates results and returns a confidence-weighted average.

You get individualized LT1 and LT2 values in pace, power, and heart rate — not a generic estimate from a formula, but thresholds derived from YOUR lactate response.

## How It Works

### Step 1: Paste Your Data

Enter your step test results: each stage's average pace (or power), heart rate, and blood lactate concentration. The tool accepts running (min/km or min/mi), cycling (watts), and swimming data. Minimum 5 stages required; 7-10 stages recommended for highest accuracy.

### Step 2: Automatic Analysis

The engine applies three detection methods in parallel:

- **ModDmax (Bishop 1998):** Finds the maximum distance between your lactate curve and a straight-line reference. Reliable across all athlete profiles, including well-trained athletes where classic Dmax fails.
- **Baseline Rise (Faude 2009):** Detects LT1 as the first sustained rise above resting lactate (+0.5 mmol/L). LT2 identified at the inflection where lactate accumulation accelerates beyond clearance capacity.
- **Sustained Increase:** Identifies the point where each successive stage produces a consistently higher lactate value — the hallmark of threshold crossing.

Results are aggregated using median pace/power/HR (ensuring the output matches a real measured point) and mean lactate values.

### Step 3: Review Your Results

The analyzer returns a complete threshold report with a confidence score from 0 to 1. High confidence (>0.75) means all three methods agree. Lower confidence flags potential data quality issues or ambiguous curves.

## The Model: Why This Is Different

Most race prediction tools use a single formula: plug in one race time, get another. The most popular — Daniels' VDOT — is a statistical regression. It works on average, but it systematically over-predicts marathons for "fast-twitch" athletes and under-predicts for "diesel" athletes.

Lactate Lab uses the **di Prampero (2003) energy cost model**, which accounts for the actual physiological determinants of endurance performance:

1. **Energy cost of locomotion** — how many mL of O2 you spend per kilometer at race pace
2. **VO2max** — your ceiling for aerobic energy production (estimated from your LT2 and heart rate data via Swain & ACSM)
3. **Fractional utilization** — what percentage of VO2max you can sustain at each distance (determined by your LT2)
4. **VLamax** — your rate of glycolytic energy production, estimated from your LT1/LT2 ratio

This last variable is what makes the prediction genuinely individual.

## Why VLamax Matters

Two athletes with identical VO2max values can have dramatically different optimal distances:

- **High VLamax ("glycolytic"):** Strong anaerobic contribution, burns glycogen faster, fatigues earlier at long distances. Relatively better at 5K and 10K.
- **Low VLamax ("diesel"):** Low anaerobic contribution, spares glycogen, sustains threshold pace longer. Relatively better at half marathon and marathon.

A generic calculator ignores this entirely. If you give it your 5K time, it assumes an "average" VLamax profile and predicts your marathon accordingly. For athletes at the extremes, the error can exceed 10 minutes in a marathon.

## Why %HRmax Zones Are Wrong

The "220 minus age" formula was never intended for individual prescription. It was derived from population averages with enormous individual variation. Robergs & Landwehr (2002) showed that the standard deviation is plus or minus 10-12 bpm — meaning your actual max HR could easily be 20 bpm higher or lower than predicted.

But even if you know your true max HR, zones based on %HRmax still fail because they assume everyone's LT1 and LT2 fall at the same relative intensity. They do not. A well-trained endurance athlete might have LT1 at 78% of HRmax, while a recreational runner might hit LT1 at 65% of HRmax. Setting both athletes' "Zone 2 ceiling" at 75% of HRmax means one is training too hard and the other is training too easy.

The only solution is to anchor your zones to YOUR measured thresholds.

## Frequently Asked Questions

**Which zone model is better — 3 or 5?**
The 3-zone model is simpler and sufficient for most athletes, especially those following polarized training. The 5-zone model is better for coaches prescribing specific tempo or sub-threshold work who need finer gradations between LT1 and LT2. Both are physiologically valid; the choice is about practical preference.

**Can I use heart rate only without pace or power data?**
Yes. If you only have HR from your lactate test, the calculator will generate HR-based zones. However, you will not get pace or power targets. For complete zones, we recommend recording pace (or power) during each stage of your step test.

**What if I only have cycling data — can I get running zones?**
No. Thresholds are sport-specific. Your LT2 heart rate on the bike is not the same as your LT2 heart rate while running (running HR is typically 5-10 bpm higher at threshold). You need a separate test for each discipline.

**How often should I update my zones?**
After any significant training block (6-8 weeks), after an illness or injury layoff, and at the start of each macrocycle. If your training has been consistent and you feel your zones still match your perception, extending to 10-12 weeks is reasonable.

**Is it free?**
Yes. The lactate test zones calculator is completely free, with no signup required. Enter your LT1 and LT2 values, select your zone model, and get your personalized zones immediately.

## Por Que las Zonas por %FCmax Estan Mal

La formula "220 menos edad" nunca fue pensada para prescripcion individual. Se derivo de medias poblacionales con enorme variacion individual. Robergs & Landwehr (2002) demostraron que la desviacion estandar es de mas/menos 10-12 ppm — lo que significa que tu FC maxima real podria ser facilmente 20 ppm mas alta o mas baja de lo predicho.

Pero incluso si conoces tu FC maxima real, las zonas basadas en %FCmax siguen fallando porque asumen que el LT1 y LT2 de todo el mundo caen en la misma intensidad relativa. No es asi. Un atleta de resistencia bien entrenado podria tener LT1 al 78% de FCmax, mientras que un corredor recreativo podria alcanzar LT1 al 65% de FCmax. Poner el "techo de Zona 2" de ambos atletas al 75% de FCmax significa que uno entrena demasiado fuerte y el otro demasiado suave.

La unica solucion es anclar tus zonas a TUS umbrales medidos.

## Dos Modelos de Zonas

### Modelo de 3 Zonas (Seiler)

Basado en la investigacion de Stephen Seiler sobre distribucion de intensidad en atletas de resistencia de elite. Simple, practico y respaldado por decadas de evidencia sobre entrenamiento polarizado.

| Zona | Nombre | Definicion | Que Pasa Fisiologicamente |
|---|---|---|---|
| Z1 | Baja intensidad | Por debajo de LT1 | Completamente aerobico. Lactato estable cerca del basal. Ritmo conversacional. |
| Z2 | Umbral | LT1 a LT2 | Metabolismo mixto. Lactato subiendo pero manejable. "Comodamente duro." |
| Z3 | Alta intensidad | Por encima de LT2 | Lactato acumulandose mas rapido que el aclaramiento. Tiempo limitado. |

Ideal para atletas que siguen una distribucion de entrenamiento polarizada o piramidal.

### Modelo de 5 Zonas

Un desglose mas granular para entrenadores que quieren mayor control en la prescripcion:

| Zona | Nombre | Definicion |
|---|---|---|
| Z1 | Recuperacion / Facil | Por debajo del 90% de LT1 |
| Z2 | Resistencia Aerobica | 90% de LT1 a LT1 |
| Z3 | Tempo | LT1 a 90% de LT2 |
| Z4 | Umbral | 90% de LT2 a LT2 |
| Z5 | VO2max+ | Por encima de LT2 |

Ideal para planes de entrenamiento estructurados con sesiones especificas de tempo, umbral y VO2max.

## Preguntas Frecuentes

**Que modelo de zonas es mejor — 3 o 5?**
El modelo de 3 zonas es mas simple y suficiente para la mayoria de atletas, especialmente los que siguen entrenamiento polarizado. El modelo de 5 zonas es mejor para entrenadores que prescriben trabajo especifico de tempo o sub-umbral y necesitan gradaciones mas finas entre LT1 y LT2. Ambos son fisiologicamente validos; la eleccion es de preferencia practica.

**Puedo usar solo frecuencia cardiaca sin datos de ritmo o potencia?**
Si. Si solo tienes FC de tu test de lactato, la calculadora generara zonas basadas en FC. Sin embargo, no obtendras objetivos de ritmo o potencia. Para zonas completas, recomendamos registrar el ritmo (o potencia) durante cada escalon de tu test.

**Si solo tengo datos de ciclismo, puedo obtener zonas de carrera?**
No. Los umbrales son especificos del deporte. Tu FC de LT2 en bici no es la misma que tu FC de LT2 corriendo (la FC de carrera es tipicamente 5-10 ppm mas alta en umbral). Necesitas un test separado para cada disciplina.

**Cada cuanto debo actualizar mis zonas?**
Despues de cualquier bloque de entrenamiento significativo (6-8 semanas), despues de una baja por enfermedad o lesion, y al inicio de cada macrociclo. Si tu entrenamiento ha sido consistente y sientes que tus zonas aun coinciden con tu percepcion, extender a 10-12 semanas es razonable.
